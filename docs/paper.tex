\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We study credit assignment for LLM agents that act through code over multiple turns. Standard outcome-reward RL samples one completion and broadcasts a single scalar advantage across every generated token: workable when the trajectory is one block of text, coarse when an early probe or draft changes the information available to later turns. Decisions whose effect passes through external state, tool outputs, and subsequent prompts then receive the wrong credit signal.

We propose a critic-free estimator that branches rollouts at turn boundaries. For each prompt the model samples several first-turn variants and several continuations under each variant; leaf rewards define a root Monte Carlo baseline for the prompt and a per-prefix baseline for each first-turn variant, so first-turn tokens are credited only when their prefix improves the expected reward of downstream continuations. Paired with token-summed clipped policy gradients, the estimator admits a per-turn loss decomposition that recovers the exact full-trajectory gradient while substantially reducing peak activation memory.

We instantiate the method on a two-turn code-policy task in which a first-turn probe can identify a hidden environment parameter the final policy needs, and lay out a staged ablation that separates the per-turn credit signal from branching, from multi-turn training, and from single-turn RL. Experiments are pending.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern outcome-reward RL for LLMs samples a completion, scores it, and broadcasts a single advantage across every generated token. When the completion is one block of text, that approximation is at worst coarse: each token influences later tokens only by extending the prefix. When the trajectory contains environment probes, scratchpad programs, tool calls, and drafts whose effects reach later turns through external state, it can be wrong. An early turn that gathers information is rewarded only if the final answer happens to succeed; a final answer that succeeds despite an uninformative early turn rewards the early turn anyway. The trajectory's structure --- which turns matter, and through what channel --- is invisible to the gradient.

We study this in code-policy generation. The agent is given an environment interface, interacts for two chat turns, and emits a Python \texttt{policy(state)} function. Only this final policy is executed and scored; the deployed object is a program, not an LLM queried online. The first turn is free: scratchpad arithmetic, environment probes, draft policies. Useful first-turn behavior receives credit only through its effect on the second turn. The setting borrows the executable-artifact framing of FunSearch and AlphaEvolve and the broader motivation of AI-Scientist-style discovery loops~\cite{romera-paredes2024funsearch,novikov2025alphaevolve,lu2024aiscientist}, but isolates the credit-assignment problem inside one prompt rather than building a full discovery system.

The concrete instance is probe-and-adapt control of a seeded batch crystallizer. The policy sets the cooling rate; the fastest safe recipe rides just below a hidden metastable limit, and crossing the limit ruins the batch. The limit leaves no trace in the readings until it is crossed, so a first-turn program can find it only by crossing it on a throwaway batch; a second-turn policy can then ride just below it. If the final policy succeeds, a flat reward cannot say whether the probe was informative or whether the second-turn code happened to work without it.

We propose a critic-free Monte Carlo estimator for this situation. For each prompt the model samples a small tree: several first-turn variants, and several second-turn continuations below each variant. The mean reward over the whole tree estimates the prompt's root value; the mean below one variant estimates that prefix's value. Their difference advantages the first-turn tokens; the gap between a leaf reward and its prefix value advantages the second-turn tokens. The estimator pairs with a per-turn loss decomposition for token-summed clipped surrogates: because the loss is additive over disjoint token sets when all pieces share a global normalizer, one forward/backward per turn recovers the exact full-trajectory gradient at substantially reduced peak activation memory.

We instantiate the method on a custom control task in which a first-turn probe can reveal a hidden safety limit that the final policy needs. Experiments are pending.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a critic-free turn-level Monte Carlo baseline with per-turn advantages for sparse-reward multi-turn RL;
  \item an exact per-turn loss decomposition for token-summed clipped policy-gradient objectives;
  \item a probe-and-adapt benchmark and an ablation design isolating the per-turn credit signal.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Sparse-reward multi-turn LLM agents.}
Recent work treats LLM agents as sequential decision-makers rather than single completions. ArCHer uses learned turn-level values for language agents~\cite{zhou2024archer}. RAGEN/StarPO studies trajectory-level reinforcement learning for interactive agents~\cite{wang2025ragen}. Agent Lightning casts arbitrary agent executions as MDP trajectories with a credit-assignment module~\cite{luo2025agentlightning}. Other work adds explicit turn-level rewards to multi-turn PPO or GRPO~\cite{zeng2025turnlevel}. These approaches address the same failure mode: a final reward alone is too coarse to say which turn mattered. Our method differs by estimating turn-level baselines from final rewards alone, without a learned critic or manually designed intermediate reward.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo credit assignment without critics.}
GRPO replaces PPO's learned critic with group-relative Monte Carlo baselines~\cite{shao2024deepseekmath}. Token-summed variants such as DAPO modify the normalization for long language-model completions~\cite{yu2025dapo}. These methods are critic-free, but a flat group baseline still assigns the same advantage to every token in a trajectory. VinePPO addresses a related issue by re-branching from intermediate reasoning states and estimating values with Monte Carlo continuations~\cite{kazemnejad2024vineppo}. Our estimator is the turn-level analogue: the branch points are chat/tool boundaries, and the resulting scalar advantage is broadcast to the tokens of the corresponding turn.

\paragraph{Executable discovery and code generation.}
FunSearch, AlphaEvolve, and related systems show that LLMs can search over executable programs using automated evaluators~\cite{romera-paredes2024funsearch,novikov2025alphaevolve}. Work on automatic discovery of RL algorithms broadens this view to machine-designed update rules~\cite{oh2025discorl}. CodeRL, RLTF, and SWE-RL use execution or rule-based feedback to train code generators~\cite{le2022coderl,liu2023rltf,wei2025swerl}. ReVeal is closer to our setting because it uses multi-turn code generation with tool feedback and dense per-turn rewards~\cite{jin2025reveal}. Our setting is different in that the first turn is valuable primarily as information gathering: it may reveal a hidden environment parameter that the final policy should exploit.

\paragraph{Scientific agents.}
AI-Scientist-style systems use LLMs to generate hypotheses, write and run code, analyze results, and draft papers~\cite{lu2024aiscientist}. These systems motivate our setting, but they operate at a higher level of abstraction. We study a smaller trainable component: how to assign credit inside a multi-turn, tool-mediated interaction when only the final executable artifact receives a reward.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The agent is a language model $\pi_\theta$ over chat-template
trajectories. The base is \texttt{Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507} with
frozen weights $W$; $\theta$ parameterizes a LoRA adapter
$W' = W + AB^\top$. A task $\mathcal{T}$ specifies environment source
code, $S$ scenarios $\{\sigma_i\}$ that vary the seed and hidden
parameters, and a per-scenario reward. The scoring oracle runs a
candidate policy body in a sandboxed subprocess against each scenario
and returns
\(
  R(\text{code};\mathcal{T}) = \sum_i r_i(\text{code};\sigma_i).
\)
Validation failures, errors, and timeouts on $\sigma_i$ score the
\emph{error floor} $r_i = -200$, calibrated below any
running-but-failing trajectory --- otherwise errored rollouts ($r=0$)
outrank running-but-poor rollouts and the gradient rewards crashing.

\subsection{Chat-template protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Each turn the model emits one fenced code block tagged \texttt{policy}
or \texttt{main}. \texttt{policy} defines \texttt{def policy(state)},
which the tool executes across all scenarios and reports the summed
reward as a user-role message. \texttt{main} is scratchpad or probe
code run in a rehearsal sandbox; the tool returns truncated stdout but
no final policy reward. The final turn must be \texttt{policy};
\texttt{main} or untagged final turns are floored. Malformed intermediate turns are likewise
floored and surfaced as a textual rejection. A length-$N$ trajectory
is
\(
  \tau = (s,\, y^{(1)},\, m^{(1)},\, \dots,\, y^{(N-1)},\, m^{(N-1)},\, y^{(N)}),
\)
with completions $y^{(n)}$ alternating with tool messages $m^{(n)}$,
and $R(\tau) = R(\text{code in }y^{(N)})$ the only scalar reward.
The remainder of the paper specializes to $N{=}2$: a single
intermediate turn (draft policy or scratchpad) followed by a committed
\texttt{policy}. Generalizing the rollout, advantages, and loss
decomposition below to $N{>}2$ is straightforward (see
\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Token-summed clipped surrogate}

For trajectory $b$ at position $t$, with importance ratio
$\rho^b_t = \pi_\theta / q$ and per-token advantage $A^b_t$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) =
 -\frac{1}{N_{\text{tok}}}\sum_{b,t}
   \mathbf{1}\{\text{model token}\}\cdot
   \min\!\big(\rho^b_t A^b_t,\,\mathrm{clip}(\rho^b_t,\,1-\epsilon_{\text{lo}},\,1+\epsilon_{\text{hi}}) A^b_t\big),
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with $N_{\text{tok}}$ the count of model tokens in the global batch.\footnote{This
matches the DAPO and CISPO recipes; per-row averaging variants
(original GRPO, BNPO, Dr.GRPO) divide by per-row token counts.}
\eqref{eq:loss} is \emph{additive over disjoint token subsets}: for
any partition of model-token positions, the loss equals the sum of
partial losses computed with the same $N_{\text{tok}}$. This is the
property the per-turn decomposition (\S\ref{sec:per-turn-decomp}) exploits.

\subsection{Branched rollout}

For each prompt $s$, sample $G_1$ turn-1 variants in one
\texttt{model.generate} call:
$y^{(1)}_i \sim q(\cdot\mid s)$, $i=1,\dots,G_1$. For each $y^{(1)}_i$
execute the tool to obtain $m_i$, then sample $G_2$ continuations
$y^{(2)}_{i,j} \sim q(\cdot\mid s, y^{(1)}_i, m_i)$ in one call. Score
each leaf to obtain $\{R_{i,j}\}$. Generate-call cost per prompt is
$1+G_1$, versus $2G_1 G_2$ for an unbranched rollout with the same
number of leaves.

\subsection{Per-turn MC value estimates and advantages}

Two empirical values, both pure averages over the tree:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat V_0 = \frac{1}{G_1 G_2}\sum_{i,j} R_{i,j},
  \qquad
  \widehat V_1^{(i)} = \frac{1}{G_2}\sum_{j} R_{i,j}.
\end{equation}
$\widehat V_0$ estimates the initial-state value;
$\widehat V_1^{(i)}$ the value after committing to turn-1 variant
$y^{(1)}_i$. Per-turn advantages, broadcast to every token of the
corresponding turn:
\begin{align}
  A^{(1)}_{i}      &= \widehat V_1^{(i)} - \widehat V_0
    & \text{(over $y^{(1)}_i$)}, \label{eq:adv1}\\
  A^{(2)}_{i,j}    &= R_{i,j} - \widehat V_1^{(i)}
    & \text{(over $y^{(2)}_{i,j}$).} \label{eq:adv2}
\end{align}
The turn-2 advantage uses the conditional mean given the turn-1
prefix rather than the global per-prompt mean: a poor turn-2 sample
drawn from a strong turn-1 is recognized as poor instead of
inheriting its prefix's above-average outcome. Tool-message tokens
are masked via \texttt{tool\_mask}; their advantage is zero.
Figure~\ref{fig:credit-assignment} contrasts this scheme with the
two closest critic-free / per-turn-credit baselines for multi-turn
LLM RL.

\subsection{Per-turn loss decomposition}
\label{sec:per-turn-decomp}

The token-summed clipped surrogate \eqref{eq:loss} factors over any
partition of the model-token positions into disjoint subsets that
share the same global normalizer $N_{\text{tok}}$. Letting
$\mathcal{I}_n$ index the model tokens of turn $n$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}_n},
  \qquad N_{\text{tok}} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} |\mathcal{I}_n|.
\label{eq:additive}
\end{equation}
Per-row averaged variants (original GRPO, BNPO, Dr.GRPO) do
\emph{not} factor this way: each row carries its own denominator,
so the partial sums do not combine into the single-row loss. The
token-summed form is what makes the decomposition exact.

\eqref{eq:additive} is exploited to compute $\mathcal{L}$ in $N$
forward/backward passes rather than one. Pass $n$ forwards
$[s \,|\, y^{(1)} \,|\, m^{(1)} \,|\, \dots \,|\, y^{(n)}]$,
computes $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}_n}$, backwards, and frees. By
\eqref{eq:additive} the parameter gradients accumulated across the
$N$ passes equal the single-pass gradient exactly.

\paragraph{Why this saves memory.} The dominant activation cost is
the LM-head logit tensor ($|V| = 151{,}936$). A single
forward+backward over the full trajectory holds the saved-for-backward
LM-head tensors at all $\sum_n T_n$ model-token positions
simultaneously; doing one forward+backward per turn holds at most
$\max_n T_n$ positions at peak, and \eqref{eq:additive} guarantees
the accumulated gradient is identical. This is gradient accumulation
at the turn level within a trajectory --- the saving is a factor of
roughly $N$ for equal-length turns, $\approx 2\times$ at our $N{=}2$
settings. Appendix~\ref{app:memory} works through the accounting.

\paragraph{Restrictions.} The decomposition's exactness has one
\emph{conceptual} requirement and two \emph{implementation}
requirements. Conceptual: the token-summed normalizer of
\eqref{eq:loss} --- per-row averaged variants do not factor across
token-position partitions, as discussed above. Implementation:
$\beta=0$ (a KL term to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ would require per-segment
slicing of reference log-probabilities) and
\texttt{num\_iterations}$=1$ (so cached
\texttt{old\_per\_token\_logps} need no re-segmenting). The two
implementation requirements can be lifted with extra bookkeeping at
no theoretical cost (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Experimental setup}

\paragraph{Environment.} We use a seeded batch cooling crystallizer rather than a standard benchmark whose solution may already be familiar to the base model. A saturated solution holding a known mass of seed crystals is cooled from $55$--$65\,^\circ$C to a target of $18$--$25\,^\circ$C. The policy observes $\{t,T,c,L,T_{\rm target},\text{steps\_left}\}$ (time, temperature, dissolved concentration, mean crystal size, target) and outputs the jacket rate as a fraction of its $1.5\,^\circ$C/min limit, clipped to $[-1,1]$, once per 20\,s step for at most 600 steps. Cooling creates supersaturation $\sigma=c/c^*(T)-1$; the seeds grow and consume it. Success requires reaching the target temperature with the solution equilibrated ($\sigma\le0.02$). If $\sigma$ ever exceeds the hidden metastable limit $\sigma_{\max}\sim U[0.08,0.25]$, a shower of new nuclei ruins the batch and the episode ends in failure. The reward is $-0.1$ per step, $+120$ on success and $-80$ on failure. Full dynamics are in Appendix~\ref{app:crystallizer}.

\paragraph{Why it tests turn-level credit.} The time-optimal recipe rides just below $\sigma_{\max}$, but $\sigma_{\max}$ enters the dynamics only through the shower test: the readings of a batch that never showers are identical for every value of the limit. A final policy therefore cannot learn the limit in-episode, and its best blind option is to ride at the lowest limit in the range, which costs two to three times the optimal batch time at the top of the range. A first-turn probe run, which has the same environment class, the same actions and readings and the same hidden limit on a different batch, can find the limit the only way physics allows: cool at the jacket limit until the batch showers and keep the last safe supersaturation. One batch brackets the limit to about $0.01$; three batches to $2\times10^{-4}$. This makes the first turn useful only through its effect on the final policy. Whether the gap between a blind controller and a limit-aware oracle is large enough to carry the study was settled with hand-written controllers before any model was run (Stage~0, \S\ref{sec:plan}): the best blind controller reaches $7.5\%$ fast success over the range against $100\%$ for the informed one, and the hand-written probe run plus final run reaches $100\%$ fast success through the training runner (Stage~0b).

\paragraph{Model and training.} We train LoRA rank $r=16$ on attention projections of \texttt{Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507}; base weights are frozen, bf16 is used, and gradient checkpointing is enabled. Unless stated otherwise, $G_1=G_2=4$ and each turn is capped at 400 tokens. The loss is the token-summed clipped surrogate \eqref{eq:loss} with $\epsilon_{\rm lo}=0.2$, asymmetric upper clipping, $\beta=0$, no critic, no GAE, and no context truncation across turns.

\paragraph{Diagnostics.} We log $\widehat V_0$, the between-prefix and within-prefix reward standard deviations, their ratio, per-turn advantage scales, error-floor rate, and generate calls per prompt. The between/within ratio is also used diagnostically: values above one indicate that first-turn choice dominates continuation noise, where per-turn credit should help most.

\section{Experiment plan}
\label{sec:plan}

The experiments form a ladder of stages. Each stage asks one question and is a go-or-stop check for the next, so that no result is built on a step that failed. Stages 0 and 0b use hand-written controllers only; Stages 1 and 2 run the frozen base model; Stages 3 to 6 train, and correspond to arms C to F of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. Throughout, \emph{fast success} means that the final run succeeds within $1.25\times$ the time-optimal batch time (\S\ref{sec:eval}), the \emph{blind} reference is the best hand-written controller that does not know the hidden limit, and the \emph{oracle} is the controller that does. Each subsection records the stage's question, what decides its outcome, and its status; results are filled in as stages complete, with the evidence in the dated log \texttt{docs/findings.tex}.

\subsection{Stage 0: is the environment worth it?}
\textbf{Question.} Does knowing the hidden limit buy anything that a controller without it cannot get from the final run's own readings?
\textbf{Protocol.} A blind side (one hand-written controller for every limit in the range) and an informed side (one controller per limit) search the same controller families with the same optimizer budget on the same seeds, and are scored on fresh seeds. Three gates were fixed beforehand: the informed side reaches $\ge 90\%$ fast success at every limit (G1); it beats the blind side by $\ge 40$ percentage points of fast success over the range (G2); a hand-written probe run plus final run reaches $\ge 95\%$ fast success (G3).
\textbf{Status.} Passed on 2026-10-24. The best blind controller rides at the lowest limit in the range and reaches $7.5\%$ fast success (normalised time $0.50$); the informed side reaches $100\%$ at every limit; the hand-written two-turn solution reaches $100\%$, recovering the limit to $2\times10^{-4}$. The same gate had failed for the earlier slosh-tank environment, which was dropped.

\subsection{Stage 0b: the hand-written solution through the training runner}
\textbf{Question.} Does the Stage~0 solution survive the real execution path: the sandbox subprocess, the probe-run environment with the limit masked, and a probe batch different from the scoring batch?
\textbf{Protocol.} The Stage~0 probe source is pushed through the runner's turn executor, its printed bracket is pasted into the final-run template, and the final policy is scored as the trainer scores it, on the 36 task-file batches plus 200 fresh ones. Gate: fast success $\ge 95\%$.
\textbf{Status.} Passed on 2026-10-25: $100\%$ fast success on 236 batches, no probe failures, probe error $2\times10^{-4}$, normalised time $0.97$--$0.99$.

\subsection{Stage 1: the frozen model in one turn (arm A)}
\textbf{Question.} Can the base model write a working cooling policy from the prompt alone, and how fast is it without a probe run?
\textbf{Protocol.} $N$ one-turn samples per held-out task, scored exactly as in training; fast success, normalised time and reward next to the blind and oracle references.
\textbf{Decides.} The floor of the study. A rate near the blind reference shows the headroom a probe run can buy. A model that cannot write a running controller at all is a prompt problem to fix before anything else.
\textbf{Status.} Done on 2026-10-25 (12 held-out batches, 8 samples each, temperature 0.8). \textbf{Result.} The code runs in 99\% of samples, but the batch is ruined in 79 of 96: the model takes the top of the stated range as its target (the constant 0.25 appears in 92 of 96 policies) and cools too hard from the first step. Success 12.5\%, fast success 1\%, normalised time 0.075, mean reward $-61$, against $+89$ for the blind cautious controller and $+107$ for the oracle. The frozen model sits far below the blind reference, so the headroom is the whole scale: caution first, then the probe.

\subsection{Stage 2: the frozen model with a probe run (arm B)}
\textbf{Question.} Does the frozen model already write a useful probe run and use its output?
\textbf{Protocol.} As Stage~1, with two turns under the training protocol: the final turn must be a policy, and an early policy does not end the rollout. The turn-1 mode and code are kept per sample, so the record shows which probe the model writes: none, a slow creep, or a fast one.
\textbf{Decides.} If arm B is already near the informed ceiling, RL has nothing left to teach about probing and the environment must get harder before any training run (hide the growth constant too, or add a jacket lag). If arm B is near arm A, the gap between them is the training target.
\textbf{Status.} Done on 2026-10-25. \textbf{Result.} The model used the probe run in all 96 samples, but 83 probe runs only reset the environment and print its initial state, the shape of the prompt's format example; 9 step a few times at full cooling, on an environment they construct themselves with a random limit; none brackets the limit. The final policies paste numbers from the probe batch into code that runs on a different batch (93 of 96) and all contain the constant 0.25: success 3 of 96, no fast success, normalised time 0.013, mean reward $-76$, below arm A. The probe run is a ritual, not an experiment, and the risk that the frozen model already writes the one-batch probe did not materialise. The gap to the informed ceiling is the training target.

\textbf{Example ablation} (2026-10-26, config key \texttt{prompt.example}). The same protocol with three versions of the prompt's format example, 96 samples each: the original (\emph{full}: reset the environment and print the state), the same two blocks with a comment in place of the probe code (\emph{format}), and no example (\emph{none}). Full replicated the result above: 80 of 96 probe runs copy the example. None removed the probe run altogether: the model answered with a policy in all 96 first turns, 59 of them without the required fence, so they were rejected. Format removed the copying (31 of 96) and produced experiments: 61 probe runs say they will find the limit and 44 step an environment in a loop and check the end flag, but every one constructs a fresh crystallizer instead of using the one provided, 39 with a limit of the model's own choosing, so the experiment can only confirm a guess; 5 heat instead of cool; and no final policy takes a number from its probe output, 92 of 96 keep the constant 0.25 (success 7 of 96, fast 1). The probes are three times longer and 17 ran out of the 600-token turn budget. The format example is the default from here on. What the frozen model lacks is not the idea of an experiment but two facts about the given environment: that the probe must run on it rather than on a copy, and that the number found there must become the setpoint.

\paragraph{Primary metric.} Held-out \emph{fast-success} rate: the
fraction of (sample, batch) pairs in which the final run succeeds within
$1.25\times$ the time-optimal batch time for that batch and limit, on 12
held-out batches whose limits are drawn from the training range. Raw
success is not informative here, since a blind cautious policy succeeds on

outside the training range, where a fixed cautious policy is furthest from
optimal, is planned. Results are taken at the best checkpoint and reported

steps and against wall-clock. Statistical test: paired Wilcoxon over
per-seed best-checkpoint fast-success rates; report effect size, not just

\paragraph{Diagnostic checks.}
We track two mechanism checks. First, when turn~1 emits a \texttt{policy}, its reward should improve faster under F than under flat multi-turn training. Second, prompts with high iteration-0 signal ratio $\widehat\sigma(\widehat V_1^{(i)})/\overline{\widehat\sigma(R_{i,\cdot})}$ should show the largest F--D gain; otherwise the proposed credit-assignment explanation is weak.

\paragraph{Failure modes.} If F does not beat D, the hierarchical baseline is not useful in this domain. If F $\approx$ E while both beat D, the benefit comes from branching rather than turn-level credit. If C--F do not beat A/B, the protocol is better framed as prompting rather than RL training.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}

The implementation and experiments use $N=2$. Extending to more turns is conceptually direct: each boundary value becomes the conditional Monte Carlo mean over the subtree below it, and the loss decomposition applies one partial loss per turn. The cost is bookkeeping and additional forward/backward passes, not a new estimator.

The method also assumes deterministic rewards given the seed. In stochastic environments, the same estimator applies but $G_2$ must grow to separate prefix quality from environment noise. The current implementation uses $\beta=0$ and \texttt{num\_iterations}$=1$; KL penalties and cached old log-probabilities can be supported by storing the same quantities per turn segment.

A natural memory/variance extension is token subsampling within a turn. Since each turn uses one scalar advantage broadcast across its tokens, subsampling tokens is an unbiased estimator of the same token-summed gradient when $N_{\rm tok}$ counts only kept positions. We leave uniform and asymmetric subsampling variants to future work.

\section{Conclusion}

We present a critic-free, turn-level credit-assignment scheme for sparse-reward multi-turn RL. The method branches rollouts at chat-turn boundaries, uses the resulting Monte Carlo conditional means as per-turn baselines, and pairs with a per-turn loss decomposition that recovers the exact full-trajectory gradient one turn at a time.

The experiments that test this --- frozen one- and two-turn references, single-turn RL, flat trajectory-level multi-turn optimization, and branched optimization with per-turn baselines --- are pending. The estimator extends naturally to more turns and to stochastic rewards (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}); whether the per-turn signal closes the gap between a blind and an informed final policy is the headline empirical claim under test.

\section{Memory accounting and the trailing-window LM-head}
\label{app:memory}

The loss only needs logits whose successor token was emitted by the model; prompt and tool-message logits do not contribute. HF \texttt{transformers} exposes this through \texttt{logits\_to\_keep}: an integer keeps the last $K$ positions, while an index tensor selects arbitrary positions.

This reduces unnecessary logits, but a single forward/backward still holds all selected LM-head outputs until backward completes. With gradient checkpointing, transformer-body activations are comparatively small; the expensive saved tensor is the LM-head output of shape $(B,K,|V|)$. Therefore, a full multi-turn backward over all model tokens scales with $K=\sum_n T_n$.

The per-turn decomposition serializes these positions. Turn $n$ forwards the prefix through $y^{(n)}$, computes the partial token-summed loss for that turn using the global $N_{\rm tok}$, backpropagates, and frees the graph. By additivity of \eqref{eq:loss}, the accumulated gradient equals the single-pass gradient, while the peak LM-head tensor scales with $\max_n T_n$.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Regime & Positions through LM head & Logit tensor \\
\midrule
Single pass, no \texttt{logits\_to\_keep} & $T_{\text{full}} = 1350$ & $\approx 13.1$\,GB \\
Single pass, selected model tokens & $\sum_n T_n = 800$ & $\approx 7.8$\,GB \\
Two turn-wise passes & $400$ per pass, freed between & $\approx 3.9$\,GB \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Arithmetic LM-head logit sizes for $B=16$, $|V|\approx 152{,}000$, and 4-byte logits. Total GPU memory also includes weights, optimizer state, body activations, KV cache, and allocator overhead.}
\label{tab:memory}
\end{table}

The decomposition is not a new \texttt{logits\_to\_keep} trick; all regimes use the same primitive. The non-trivial point is that token-summed normalization makes the per-turn gradients sum exactly to the full-trajectory gradient. Per-row-averaged variants would require either a different normalizer or an approximation.

\paragraph{Forward-side temporaries change the binding constraint.}
Table~\ref{tab:memory} accounts for the LM-head logits tensor as it appears in the saved-for-backward computation graph. At production sizing on a 24\,GB card, the binding allocation is not the gradient at backward but the forward itself. The softmax-stability convention in TRL holds logits in fp32 rather than bf16, doubling the byte budget over a naive estimate. Worse, the temperature scaling \texttt{logits = logits / self.temperature} is not in place: a same-shape fresh tensor is allocated before the original is freed. At the divide step \emph{two} $(B, T, |V|)$ fp32 tensors are simultaneously live, $\approx 7.8$\,GiB at our production sizing. Stacked on model weights, the just-emitted turn-$n$ KV cache from generation that has not yet been reclaimed by the allocator, and the previous turn's backward residue, this allocation crosses the 22\,GiB usable budget and OOMs at step~0.

The per-turn loss decomposition addresses peak \emph{gradient} memory but does not shrink the forward logits tensor. The remedy is row-wise chunking of the per-turn pass: split the $B$ trajectories into sub-batches of size $b\!\ll\!B$, forward each sub-batch separately, backpropagate, and accumulate. Because the token-summed loss \eqref{eq:loss} is additive over disjoint row subsets sharing the same global normalizer $N_{\rm tok}$, the chunked gradient is bit-equivalent to the single-pass gradient. The forward-time logits tensor shrinks linearly from $(B, T_n, |V|)$ to $(b, T_n, |V|)$, and the divide-step peak scales correspondingly. At $b=4$, $B=16$, $T=400$ the divide-step peak drops from $\approx 7.8$\,GiB to $\approx 2$\,GiB; the cost is $B/b$ additional LM-head kernel launches per turn (single-digit-percent wall-clock at our generation-dominated sizing). We use $b=4$ on 24\,GB L4 and run un-chunked on $\geq 40$\,GB cards.

\section{Seeded cooling crystallizer}
\label{app:crystallizer}

One kilogram of solvent holds dissolved solute at concentration $c$ (kg per kg solvent) and $N$ monodisperse seed crystals of size $L$. The solubility is $c^*(T)=0.20+0.006\,T+0.0001\,T^2$ with $T$ in $^\circ$C and the relative supersaturation is $\sigma=c/c^*(T)-1$. A batch starts saturated at $T_0\sim U[55,65]\,^\circ$C with a seed mass equal to $3$--$6\%$ of the theoretical yield down to the target $T_{\rm target}\sim U[18,25]\,^\circ$C. In each 20\,s step the jacket moves the temperature by the commanded fraction of $1.5\,^\circ$C/min, the shower test is evaluated at the new temperature with the current concentration, and the crystals then grow for 20\,s:
\begin{align}
\frac{dL}{dt} &= k_g\,\sigma^{g}, \qquad k_g=1.5\times10^{-6}\,\text{m/s},\quad g=1.5, \notag\\
\frac{dc}{dt} &= -\rho N\,3L^2\,\frac{dL}{dt}, \qquad \rho=2000\,\text{kg/m}^3,
\end{align}
with dissolution by the same law when $\sigma<0$ and the mass transfer capped so that one step cannot cross saturation. Success requires $T\le T_{\rm target}+0.25\,^\circ$C and $\sigma\le0.02$. The episode ends in failure if $\sigma>\sigma_{\max}$ at any shower test (a shower of new nuclei) or if the seeds dissolve below $30\%$ of their initial size, and by timeout after 600 steps. The reward is $-0.1$ per step, $+120$ on success and $-80$ on failure.

\paragraph{Why the metastable limit is the hidden quantity.}
$\sigma_{\max}$ appears nowhere except in the shower test, so two batches with different limits produce identical readings under identical actions until one of them showers; no in-episode estimator exists. The time-optimal controller tracks $\sigma=\sigma_{\max}$ with a one-step lookahead on the solubility curve and accelerates as the crystals grow (classical programmed cooling). A controller that does not know the limit must ride at the bottom of the range ($\sigma\approx0.077$), which succeeds on every batch but takes $2$--$3$ times longer at the top of the range. The probe run finds the limit by crossing it: cooling at the jacket limit until the shower brackets $\sigma_{\max}$ to the supersaturation gained in one step, about $0.01$; two further batches that creep up from the last safe level tighten this to $2\times10^{-4}$. A slow single-batch creep is worse, not better: at high limits the growing crystals consume supersaturation faster than the creep adds it, and the batch equilibrates without ever reaching the limit.

Each training task is one batch (its seed) with its own hidden limit; the probe run uses the same limit on a different batch, and the probe-run environment exposes every method and reading of the final-run environment but not the hidden parameters. The time-optimal number of steps for the scoring batch is stored with the task and defines the fast-success and normalised-time metrics. The two-turn protocol is useful only if a probe-aware controller can close the gap: the blind cautious controller sets the no-probe reference, the oracle sets the upper bound, and the hand-written probe run plus final run is the achievable probe-aware reference.
