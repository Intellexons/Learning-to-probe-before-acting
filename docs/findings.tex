\noindent\textbf{What this is.}
A dated log of experiments run on the LoRA4VR self-play loop. Each entry
records what was tried, what happened, and how to reproduce it. Entries are
added as experiments complete. Reproduction references a git commit; the code
at that commit is the exact code that produced the result.

\noindent\textbf{System.} \texttt{Qwen2.5-Coder-3B-Instruct} fine-tuned with
GRPO/DAPO on a Google Cloud L4 GPU (24\,GB). The loop generates
\texttt{policy(state) -> action} Python code, scores it by running the policy
in a sandboxed environment, and trains LoRA adapters on the reward signal.

\section*{2026-04-13 \quad Environment design and first training runs}
% ============================================================

\commit{d96f7cb} $\cdot$ \textit{puck-on-tilted-plane env, runs 1--3}

\subsection*{Motivation}

CartPole was the original seed task but it is trivially solved by a two-line
bang-bang policy, giving a flat learning curve from iteration 0. Any standard
RL benchmark (LunarLander, Pendulum, MountainCar) is heavily represented in
the base model's pretraining data, so improvement from LoRA would be
confounded with prior knowledge. We need an environment the model has to
\emph{read} rather than recall.

\subsection*{The puck environment}

A puck moves in a bounded $[-6, 6]^2$ arena under a constant hidden tilt
$(t_x, t_y)$ and linear drag $\mu$. The policy receives
$\{p_x, p_y, v_x, v_y, g_x, g_y\}$ and must drive the puck to the goal
within 120 steps. Hidden parameters --- tilt, drag, thrust limit $a_{\max}$,
initial velocity --- are sampled per scenario from a seed and are not in the
state dict. The tight thrust envelope ($a_{\max} = \|tilt\|_2 \times 1.05 +
\epsilon$) ensures that a naive ``thrust straight at the goal'' policy
fails because it cannot cancel tilt drift.

\textbf{Reward:} $-\Delta t \cdot \text{dist}(p, g)$ per step; $+50$ bonus
on reaching the goal. Errored episodes score $-200$ per scenario (floor).

\textbf{Baseline gap} (200 seeds, hand-written policies):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Naive aim: $\approx 26\%$ goal rate
  \item PD + tilt feedforward: $\approx 73\%$ goal rate
\end{itemize}

This gap is real headroom for LoRA to exploit.

\subsection*{Bugs encountered and fixed}

Three bugs had to be resolved before any learning was possible.

\textbf{Run 1 --- OOM on L4.}
Default config ($G=16$, batch 16, max completion 900 tokens) ran out of
memory at the backward pass. Fix (PR \#6): halved $G$ and batch to 8,
trimmed completion length to 600.

\textbf{Run 2 --- Reward sign flip.}
Training ran but degraded (fewer running samples each iteration). Errored
samples scored $0.0$ while samples that ran and drifted integrated large
negative rewards ($\approx -280$). GRPO's normalized advantage placed the
$0.0$ errors \emph{above} the mean, so the trainer rewarded broken code.
Fix (PR \#7): errored scenarios score $-200$, making broken code strictly
the worst outcome.

\textbf{Run 3 --- Dead advantages.}
With LR $= 10^{-5}$ and only 1 training prompt (4 gradient steps per outer
iteration), $\approx 75\%$ of batches were uniformly errored
(advantage $= 0$, no gradient). On the remaining steps, weight deltas were
$\sim 10^{-6}$ --- indistinguishable from zero. The reward was identical to
7 decimal places at iteration 0 and iteration 9. Fix (PR \#10): LR bumped
$10\times$ to $10^{-4}$; epochs raised to 20 (20 gradient steps per outer
iteration on 1 prompt).

\subsection*{Reproduce}

Commits: OOM fix \commit{ef228fe}, reward floor \commit{ef7e0c9},
LR/epoch fix \commit{e9353d7}. Env source: \texttt{scripts/seed\_tasks/puck\_tilted\_plane\_env.py}.

\subsubsection*{Iter 0}

The base model generates varied PD/PID controllers but crashes 35\% of the
time by attempting to read hidden env internals (\texttt{a\_max}, \texttt{dt},
\texttt{steps}) from the state dict. The 13 successful policies span a reward
range of $[-92, -373]$ with genuine variation in gain choices. The best
policy uses asymmetric gains ($k_{px}=2.0, k_{dx}=1.0$; $k_{py}=1.5,
k_{dy}=1.0$) and scores $-92.4$ over 8 scenarios.

\subsubsection*{Iter 5}

Zero crashes. The error-reading habit is gone. However, all 20 samples at
temperature $0.8$ produce \emph{exactly} $-140.3516$ (identical to 4 decimal
places). Every policy is byte-for-byte the same: $k_p = 2.0$, $k_d = 1.5$.
This is the \textbf{illustrative example embedded in the prompt template}
(\texttt{configs/prompt\_template.txt}). The model has memorized and
reproduced it verbatim.

\subsection*{Interpretation}

Training achieved two things and failed at a third.

\textit{Achieved.} Error rate dropped from 35\% to 0\% --- the model learned
not to read hidden env attributes. Mean reward over runners improved by 35\%.

\textit{Failed.} The model collapsed to a single policy. With $\beta = 0$
(no KL penalty), one training prompt, and 20 gradient steps per outer
iteration, GRPO found the prompt example as a high-reward fixed point and
reinforced it into a delta distribution. By iter 5, temperature $0.8$
sampling produces zero diversity. The best reachable policy is now $-140.4$,
worse than the base model's best ($-92.4$).

This is reward hacking: the model optimized the training distribution
(memorize the example) rather than the environment.

\subsection*{Hypotheses to test}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Remove or paraphrase the prompt example.} It is the attractor
        GRPO is collapsing to. A deliberately suboptimal or absent example
        removes the fixed point.
  \item \textbf{More training prompts.} With 1 prompt, every outer iteration
        trains on the same input. Adding 4--8 tasks with varied dynamics forces
        generalization.
  \item \textbf{Raise $\beta > 0$.} A non-zero KL coefficient prevents the
        policy from drifting too far from the base distribution and preserves
        diversity.
  \item \textbf{Reduce epochs.} 20 gradient steps on 1 prompt per outer
        iteration is aggressive. 5--10 may preserve more exploration.
  \item \textbf{Probe at every intermediate checkpoint.} Does collapse happen
        at iter 1 or gradually? One probe per checkpoint would pinpoint when
        diversity dies.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Open questions}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item What does the W\&B reward curve look like across the 5 iterations?
        (The run at \texttt{puck\_grpo\_run\_5-orchestrator} has data but some
        post-resume steps may be missing due to a step-monotonicity issue.)
  \item What is the PD+feedforward ceiling expressed in probe-script reward
        units? This would establish what score a successful LoRA policy needs
        to beat.
\end{itemize}

This is the first run showing genuine learning. Three phases are visible.
\textit{Iters 0--4}: crashes drop from 85\% to 0\% as the model learns the
valid state-key constraint. \textit{Iters 4--16}: reward improves steadily

has real variance to amplify.

  \item \textbf{Measure diversity at convergence.} Run 5 collapsed to 1 unique
        policy; does run 8 maintain diversity at iter 27, or does it collapse
        to a different fixed point once reward saturates?
  \item \textbf{Compare against the hand-written ceiling.} Score the PD+feedforward
        baseline with \texttt{scripts/probe\_generation.py} at iter 0 to get
        a reward number in the same units.
  \item \textbf{Train longer or with more prompts} to see whether the plateau
        at iter 20--27 is a capacity limit or simply convergence of the single
        training task.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{2026-04-22 \quad Policy analysis: what run 8 learned, and why iter 0 failed}
% ============================================================

\commit{e6bc200} $\cdot$ \textit{manual probe inspection, run 8 iter 0 and iter 27}

\subsection*{Setup}

With run 8 showing genuine learning (mean reward $+215$ at iter 27, up from $-1403$ at iter 0),
the question was: what strategy did the model actually discover, and why was the base model unable
to write a working policy at all? All 20 iter-0 and all 20 iter-27 policy files from
\texttt{data/probe/puck\_grpo\_run\_8\_iter\{000,027\}\_t0p80/} were read and compared manually.

\subsection*{Results}

\textbf{Iter 27 --- convergent velocity P-controller.}
Every iter-27 sample uses the same structural template: compute the angle to the goal, set a
desired velocity of magnitude \textit{speed} in that direction, and command acceleration proportional
to the velocity error:
\begin{equation*}
  a = \text{gain} \times (v_{\text{desired}} - v_{\text{current}})
\end{equation*}
The only variation across the 20 samples is in two scalar hyperparameters: desired speed (1.0--5.0)
and gain ($/0.05 = \times20$, $\times10$, $\times20$). The three below-average samples each have
a distinct defect: sample 08 contains a positional-step bug that degenerates to naive aim
(reward $-62.8$); sample 10 sets speed $= 1.0$, too slow to reach the goal in 120 steps;
sample 15 self-clips at $3.0$ below the env's own $a_{\max}$.

\textbf{Iter 0 --- API mismatch causes 85\% crash rate.}
17 of 20 base-model policies crashed with KeyError at reward floor $-1600$.
The specific hidden keys accessed are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:iter0_crashes}.
The three survivors avoided hidden keys only by accident: two hardcoded thrust magnitude constants
and one hardcoded all physics parameters (\texttt{tilt\_x = 0.0}, \texttt{mu = 0.5},
\texttt{a\_max = 1.5}). The survivors scored $-223$, $-312$, and $-325$ --- all worse than
the naive aim baseline.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Hidden key} & \textbf{Samples accessing it} \\
\midrule
\texttt{a\_max} & 00, 01, 02, 03, 04, 05, 07, 08, 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, 19 \\
\texttt{tilt\_x}, \texttt{tilt\_y} & 01, 03, 04, 06, 08, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 19 \\
\texttt{mu} & 01, 04, 07, 08, 12, 15, 16 \\
\texttt{dt} & 16, 17 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Hidden state keys accessed by crashing iter-0 samples.}
\label{tab:iter0_crashes}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../data/probe/puck_grpo_run_8_analysis.png}
\caption{Run 8 learning curve across the 8 probed iterations, reproduced here for context.
The phase transition at iter 16--20 is the backdrop for the policy structure analysis above.}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Interpretation}

\textbf{What iter 27 discovered.}
The trained model found a velocity P-controller rather than the PD-on-position + tilt-feedforward
strategy used by the hand-written baseline. This is structurally different: there is no
position error term and no tilt awareness. The approach works because high-gain velocity
tracking rejects tilt disturbances reactively within a few timesteps, yielding near-ceiling
reward without explicit feedforward. The residual 8\% gap to the hand-written ceiling
($+248$ vs.\ $\approx +270$) is consistent with reactive rather than proactive tilt rejection.

\textbf{Why iter 0 failed.}
The base model's prior --- drawn from pretraining on standard RL codebases --- is that a physics
simulation exposes all its parameters through the state dict or environment attributes. 14 of 17
crashing samples attempted tilt compensation or drag cancellation that would have been correct
if the hidden parameters were accessible. The model's control-theory knowledge was largely sound;
its environment-API assumption was wrong. GRPO fixed the API assumption without teaching better
control theory, because velocity tracking was sufficient to saturate the available reward.

\subsection*{Hypotheses / next steps}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Probe the transition from position-thrust (iter 0 survivors) to velocity-tracking
        (iter 27).} At which iteration does the velocity-control template first appear?
        Checking iter-4 policies would show whether it emerges immediately after crash rate drops.
  \item \textbf{Test whether harder tilt distributions force tilt compensation.}
        With wider tilt ranges the reactive velocity-tracker may fail; GRPO would then need to
        discover an explicit feedforward term to push reward higher.
  \item \textbf{Provide the hidden parameters in the state dict and retrain.}
        If the base model already knows how to use tilt/drag/a\_max (most iter-0 samples attempt it),
        a single run with those keys exposed could achieve the hand-written ceiling immediately
        and reveal what further headroom exists beyond it.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Open questions}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Would the velocity-tracking template generalise to a new environment with different
        dynamics, or is it a memorised pattern for the puck task?
  \item The best iter-27 policy ($+247.6$) and the best iter-0 survivor ($-223$) both use
        only valid state keys. Is the iter-27 template a refinement of the iter-0 survivor
        template, or did it emerge independently under GRPO pressure?
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Reproduce}

Manual inspection at \commit{e6bc200}; policy files already present in
\texttt{data/probe/} (run 8, iter 0 and iter 27 probe directories).

\section*{2026-04-22 \quad Phase transition iter 16$\to$20: gain and speed jointly determine goal capture}
% ============================================================

\commit{cd60244} $\cdot$ \textit{manual inspection of iter016 and iter020 probe files, run 8}

\subsection*{Setup}

The run-8 learning curve shows a sharp mean-reward jump from $+28$ (iter~16) to $+199$
(iter~20). To understand the cause, all 40 policy files from the run-8 iter-16 and iter-20
probe directories were read and compared.

\subsection*{Results}

Both iterations use the velocity-tracking template without exception: no crashes, no hidden-key
reads. The difference lies entirely in the choice of two scalar hyperparameters.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Iter} & \textbf{Dominant speed} & \textbf{Dominant gain} & \textbf{Mean reward} & \textbf{Best reward} \\
\midrule
16 & 10.0 & $\times 1$ & $+28$ & $+12$ \\
20 & 2.0--5.0 & $\times 10$--$50$ & $+199$ & $+248$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

At iter 16, 14 of 20 samples set \texttt{desired\_speed = 10} and compute acceleration as the raw
velocity error (\texttt{ax = desired\_vx - vx}, gain $= 1$). Five samples compute the desired
velocity as an unnormalized position vector (\texttt{desired\_vx = speed * dx}), giving a
distance-proportional target that oscillates near the goal; these score $-53$ to $-154$.
By iter 20, all samples use speeds of 1.5--5.0 and gains of $\times 10$--$\times 50$ (via
\texttt{/ 0.05}, \texttt{* 10.0}, \texttt{* 20.0}, or chained multipliers). The one failure
at iter 20 (sample 14, reward $+0.5$) is the sole sample that reverts to \texttt{desired\_speed = 10}
with gain $\times 10$.

\subsection*{Interpretation}

The controller has no explicit distance term and does not decelerate near the goal. Goal capture
depends on two mechanisms.

\textit{Finite goal-zone radius.} The puck need only be inside the goal radius at one timestep.
At speed $v$ and $dt = 0.05$\,s, the per-step displacement is $v \cdot dt$. At 3\,m/s this is
0.15\,m, comfortably below the goal diameter ($\approx 0.5$\,m), guaranteeing detection on
a direct pass.

\textit{Tilt rejection and overshoot amplitude.} After the puck passes the goal, \texttt{angle\_to\_goal}
flips immediately; the controller commands a reversal. The overshoot amplitude before
the reversal takes effect scales as $v_{\text{desired}} / \text{gain}$: low speed and high gain
together keep the oscillation amplitude small so the puck re-enters the goal zone on the
next pass. More importantly, during the approach, a lateral tilt acceleration $\mathbf{g}_\perp$
induces a sideways velocity error $\delta v$. With gain $= 1$, the correction is $\delta v$
(which clips immediately at $a_{\max}$, providing no extra correction). With gain $= 20$,
the correction is $20\,\delta v$ in the linear regime, rejecting tilt drift $20\times$ faster
and keeping the trajectory aligned with the goal despite hidden tilt.

The iter-16 failure is therefore not discrete-timestep miss (step size 0.2\,m at terminal
velocity $\approx 4$\,m/s is still inside the goal diameter) but poor tilt rejection: with
gain $= 1$ the puck drifts off the goal axis and misses the zone on most scenarios. GRPO
resolved this by selectively reinforcing samples where the gain happened to be high enough
to overcome tilt drift, receiving the sharp $+50$ goal bonus as the reward signal.

\subsection*{Hypotheses / next steps}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Probe iter 17--19} to determine whether the gain shift happens in one step
        or gradually. The $+50$ bonus is a discontinuous signal that could cause a single-iteration
        jump if the right (speed, gain) combination appears at random and is then strongly reinforced.
  \item \textbf{Measure whether gain or speed is the primary driver.} Evaluate policies with
        high gain + high speed, and low gain + low speed, on the 8-scenario test set to
        separate the tilt-rejection and step-size effects.
  \item \textbf{Test with wider tilt distributions.} If tilt rejection is the bottleneck,
        harder tilt scenarios should widen the gap between gain $= 1$ and gain $= 20$
        controllers, making the transition more pronounced.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Open questions}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item The transition occurs at a specific GRPO iteration. Is there a single lucky sample
        with high gain that crossed the reward threshold and was amplified, or did gain drift
        upward continuously across iter 16--20?
  \item What is the minimum gain needed for reliable goal capture at a given tilt magnitude?
        This would give a quantitative criterion for when the velocity-tracking controller is
        ``good enough.''
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Reproduce}

Manual inspection at \commit{cd60244}; policy files already present in
\texttt{data/probe/} (run 8, iter 16 and iter 20 probe directories).

\section*{2026-04-27 \quad Branched-rollout N=2 OOMs on L4; plan to decompose loss per turn}
% ============================================================

\commit{8b94cc7} $\cdot$ \textit{branched-N=2 GRPO smoke, PR \#16}

\subsection*{Setup / motivation}

After run 8 produced a clean single-turn baseline, the next experiment is N=2 multi-turn
GRPO with branched MC rollouts ($G_1$ first-turn variants, $G_2$ continuations each), using
turn-2 mean reward as an empirical $\hat V_1$ to give turn-1 a credit-assignment signal
without a learned critic. The override (\texttt{src/branched\_trainer.py}) plugs into TRL
0.28's \texttt{GRPOTrainer} by replacing \texttt{\_generate\_and\_score\_completions}
and concatenating each trajectory as $[\text{prompt} \mid \text{turn1} \mid \text{tool} \mid \text{turn2}]$
before handing it to the parent's \texttt{\_compute\_loss}. The smoke goal was an
end-to-end GPU run on L4 (24\,GB) to validate plumbing before scaling.

\subsection*{Results}

Three smoke attempts on L4, all OOMed at the same point: a 4.06\,GiB allocation
inside the autograd backward pass, $\approx 3$\,min 30\,s into the run. The allocation
size was identical across attempts despite progressive shrinking of the config:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Attempt} & $G_1$ & $G_2$ & \texttt{max\_turn\_tokens} & \textbf{Outcome} \\
\midrule
1 (\commit{50825d3}) & 4 & 2 & 400 & OOM at backward, 4.06\,GiB \\
2 (\commit{59f4d81}) & 2 & 2 & 200 & OOM at backward, 4.06\,GiB \\
3 (\commit{f735d1d}) & 2 & 2 & 100 & OOM at backward, 4.06\,GiB \\
4 (\commit{8b94cc7}) & 2 & 2 & 200 & OOM at backward, 4.06\,GiB (prompt trimmed) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Trimming the Multi-Turn Protocol prose from the prompt freed $\approx 300$\,MiB at peak
(free memory $2.98 \to 3.29$\,GiB) but did not move the 4.06\,GiB allocation. Generation,
sandbox execution, advantage computation, tool/loss-mask assembly, prompt-completion
concatenation, and logp computation all completed --- the override reaches \texttt{loss.backward()}
on every attempt before failing.

\subsection*{Interpretation}

The plumbing is sound; the structural issue is the loss layout. The 4.06\,GiB allocation
is the logits gradient tensor at backward, shape $(B, T_{\text{completion}}, V)$ with
$V = 151{,}936$. The branched-N=2 setup makes both factors larger than single-turn:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Per-row sequence is now $[\text{prompt} \mid \text{turn1} \mid \text{tool} \mid \text{turn2}]$,
        with the prompt itself enlarged by the multi-turn protocol description, scenarios,
        and env code (single-turn prompts were $\approx 500$ tokens; branched prompts
        approach 1500--2000).
  \item The completion now spans turn-1 + tool result + turn-2 rather than a single turn.
  \item Backward retains activations across the full sequence at every transformer layer
        (36 layers, $H = 2048$); attention activations grow super-linearly in $T$.
  \item All $G_1 G_2$ trajectories run in one forward+backward graph, so memory peaks
        as a sum, not a max.
\end{itemize}

The single-turn G=16 baseline fit because per-row $T \approx 900$ and the prompt was
short. Branched G=4 does not fit because per-row $T \approx 2500$ and the multi-turn
prompt is large.

\subsection*{Hypotheses / next steps}

The next task is \textit{Option C: per-turn loss decomposition}, recorded in detail in
the tracker task \texttt{nontrivial-envs/per-turn-loss-decomposition}.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Split the forward into two passes.} Turn-1 logps need only
        $[\text{prompt} \mid \text{turn1}]$ ($G_1$ short forwards, no $G_2$ redundancy).
        Turn-2 logps still need $[\text{prompt} \mid \text{turn1} \mid \text{tool} \mid \text{turn2}]$
        but use \texttt{logits\_to\_keep} so the LM head only materializes
        $(B, T_{\text{turn2}}, V)$ rather than the full completion span.
  \item \textbf{Backward each pass separately.} Two sequential \texttt{.backward()} calls
        with \texttt{retain\_graph=False} accumulate gradients into the same parameters;
        peak memory becomes $\max$ of the two passes rather than their sum.
  \item \textbf{Custom \texttt{\_compute\_loss}.} Re-implement the DAPO loss segment-by-segment
        ($\approx 30$ lines) since TRL's parent expects one batched advantage tensor.
        Use the additive decomposition $\mathcal{L}_{ij} = \mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{ij} + \mathcal{L}^{(2)}_{ij}$
        with the $G_2$ scaling on turn-1 to match the original sum.
  \item \textbf{Optionally micro-batch turn-2} below $G_1 G_2$ to bound peak further if
        production sizing ($G_1 = G_2 = 4$, $T = 400$) still OOMs after the split.
\end{enumerate}

Memory analysis (back-of-envelope, dominant logits-grad term only, at production sizing
$G_1 = G_2 = 4$, $T_1 = T_2 = 400$, $T_t = 100$): current implementation
$\approx 4.4$\,GiB; Option C peak $\approx \max(0.49, 1.94) = 1.94$\,GiB. With turn-2
micro-batching at $b = 4$, peak drops to $\approx 0.49$\,GiB.

\subsection*{Open questions}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Will the segmented forward correctly reproduce the concatenated loss numerically?
        A CPU unit test on a fixed input is in the task acceptance criteria; the math
        decomposes cleanly because tool tokens have $m_t = 0$, but tokenization-seam
        offsets between $[\text{prompt} \mid \text{turn1}]$ and the full chat could shift
        boundaries and need careful handling via \texttt{apply\_chat\_template} on the
        full message list with index tracking.
  \item Does the additional forward (turn-1-only pass) cost more wall-time than it saves
        via the $G_2$-fold turn-1 redundancy reduction? Expected to be roughly neutral;
        confirm empirically.
  \item Generalization to $N \geq 3$: each turn gets its own forward+backward at
        $\prod_{k < n} G_k$ unique sequences. The pattern composes but compute grows
        multiplicatively per added turn.
\end{itemize}

\section*{2026-04-29 \quad Two-pass per-turn loss decomposition fits on L4}
% ============================================================

\commit{9a1c296} $\cdot$ \textit{Option C lands; branched-N=2 smoke completes end-to-end}

\subsection*{Setup}

Implemented Option C from the prior entry: split the single forward over
$[\text{prompt} \mid \text{turn1} \mid \text{tool} \mid \text{turn2}]$ into two
sequential passes. Pass 1 forwards $[\text{prompt} \mid \text{turn1}]$ and
backwards the turn-1 DAPO loss; pass 2 forwards the full prefix
$[\text{prompt} \mid \text{turn1} \mid \text{tool}]$ as the ``prompt'' with
turn-2 alone as the ``completion'', so the parent's
\texttt{logits\_to\_keep} becomes $T_{\text{turn2}}$ rather than
$T_{\text{completion}}$. Both passes share the same
\texttt{num\_items\_in\_batch} normalizer, making
$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}^{(1)} + \mathcal{L}^{(2)}$ exact for additive loss
types. Backward runs inside the overridden \texttt{compute\_loss}, with
\texttt{training\_step} skipping the outer
\texttt{accelerator.backward(loss)}. Restricted to \texttt{loss\_type} $\in$
\{\texttt{dapo}, \texttt{cispo}\}, token-level IS, $\beta = 0$,
\texttt{num\_iterations}\,$=$\,1; per-row averaging losses don't decompose
this way without a different normalizer scheme.

\subsection*{Results}

Smoke config (\texttt{configs/smoke\_test\_grpo\_branched.yaml}, $G_1 = G_2 = 2$,
\texttt{max\_turn\_tokens}\,$=$\,200) ran 4 training steps end-to-end on an L4
in \texttt{europe-west3-b} with exit code 0. Same config OOMs at backward on
\commit{8b94cc7} (parent of this branch) per the previous entry. The
peak-memory delta was not instrumented this run --- the binary
fits-vs-OOMs signal at identical sizing was the test.

\subsection*{Interpretation}

The dominant 4.06\,GiB allocation in the prior OOM was the LM-head logits
gradient at backward, shape $(B, T_{\text{completion}}, V)$. Splitting the
backward graph at the turn boundary makes peak memory
$\max(\text{pass}_1, \text{pass}_2)$ instead of their sum: pass 1's
activations are released before pass 2's forward begins. Since tool tokens
carry $m_t = 0$ in the loss mask, no gradient signal is lost by the split.

\subsection*{Hypotheses / next steps}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Bump $G_1, G_2$ on the production config and re-measure --- the
        memory headroom should now allow $G_1 G_2 \geq 8$ at
        $T \approx 200$ where single-pass previously OOMed.
  \item Add \texttt{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated()} logging to
        \texttt{training\_step} to quantify the reduction (currently the
        win is inferred, not measured).
  \item Lift the \texttt{num\_iterations}\,$=$\,1 restriction by slicing
        \texttt{old\_per\_token\_logps} per segment with the same
        right-aligned layout as the segment forwards.
  \item Per-segment \texttt{ref\_per\_token\_logps} plumbing to support
        $\beta \neq 0$ (KL term).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Open questions}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Wall-time delta vs. single-pass: two forwards instead of one, but
        the turn-1 forward is much shorter. Net unmeasured.
  \item Generalization to $N \geq 3$ turns: the additive decomposition
        composes, but each added turn adds another forward+backward whose
        prefix grows.
\end{itemize}

\section*{2026-05-09 \quad Slosh-tank env introduced; dimensionless analysis guides parameter selection}
% ============================================================

\textit{New environment, branch \texttt{main}; env source \texttt{scripts/seed\_tasks/slosh\_tank\_env.py}}

\subsection*{Decision: pivot from harder-puck to slosh tank}

The planned harder-puck variant (small $\mu=0.05$, wider tilt, tight headroom,
two-state policy interface) was abandoned before any code was committed. The
core problem: the signal of interest --- tilt estimation from transition
residuals --- requires the model to compute $(v_{\rm new}-v_{\rm prev})/dt -
a \approx \text{tilt}$, a mathematical identity. The environment cannot test
\emph{how well} the model uses that identity because a policy that writes it
verbatim from the prompt succeeds trivially. There is no gradient to climb.

The slosh-tank replaces it. The hidden unknowns affect \emph{how} to control
--- deadband changes command style, delay changes timing, slosh length changes
feedback gain tuning --- and no single formula bridges the gap. The probe
reveals the unknown; the model must then write a qualitatively adapted
controller. This is a harder reasoning task with a clearer reward surface.

\subsection*{Environment summary}

A 1D cart ($x$, $v$) carries liquid modelled as one damped pendulum mode
($\theta$, $\omega$). Policy commands horizontal acceleration, clipped to
$[-1, 1]$\,m/s$^2$. Hidden per-scenario parameters:
actuator gain $\kappa$, action delay $D$ (steps), deadband $b$,
slosh length $L$, damping $c$. Observed state:
$\{t, x, v, \theta, \omega, g, \text{steps\_left}\}$.
Dynamics: cart motion plus a forced damped pendulum with coupling
$\dot\omega = -(g/L)\theta - c\,\omega - a_{\rm true}$.
Success requires all four conditions: $|x-g|<0.12$, $|v|<0.12$,
$|\theta|<0.07$, $|\omega|<0.18$. The four-condition criterion prevents
degenerate solutions (reach position without settling liquid).
Episode length: $N_{\rm steps} = 105$, $\Delta t = 0.06$\,s (6.3\,s total).

Source: \texttt{scripts/seed\_tasks/slosh\_tank\_env.py}.
Difficulty ladder and curriculum: \texttt{slosh\_tank\_difficulty\_plan.md}.

\subsection*{Dimensionless analysis via Buckingham $\pi$}

We applied the Buckingham $\pi$ theorem directly to the physical
parameters, setting aside the integration step, success thresholds, and
RL reward weights (those belong to the measurement and training layers,
not the dynamics).

\paragraph{The 8 governing parameters.}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
\toprule
Parameter & Symbol & Value & Dimension \\
\midrule
Gravitational accel.    & $g$              & 9.81\,m\,s$^{-2}$        & $\mathsf{L\,T}^{-2}$ \\
Max commanded accel.    & $a_{\max}$        & 1.00\,m\,s$^{-2}$        & $\mathsf{L\,T}^{-2}$ \\
Cart$\to$slosh coupling & $\eta$           & 1.00\,m$^{-1}$           & $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$    \\
Slosh pendulum length   & $L$              & 0.70\,m                  & $\mathsf{L}$         \\
Slosh damping rate      & $c$              & 0.06\,s$^{-1}$           & $\mathsf{T}^{-1}$    \\
Goal distance           & $D_g$            & ${\sim}1.8$\,m           & $\mathsf{L}$         \\
Episode time            & $T_{\rm ep}$     & 6.3\,s                   & $\mathsf{T}$         \\
Actuator gain (hidden)  & $\kappa$         & $\{0.65,0.80,1.00\}$     & $1$                  \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Buckingham $\pi$ (repeating vars: $a_{\max}$, $D_g$).}
7 dimensional params, 2 fundamental dims $\Rightarrow$ 5 new groups $+$ $\kappa$:

\[
  \pi_1 = \frac{g}{a_{\max}} = 9.81,\quad
  \pi_2 = \eta D_g = 1.80,\quad
  \pi_3 = \frac{L}{D_g} = 0.39,\quad
  \pi_4 = c\sqrt{\frac{D_g}{a_{\max}}} = 0.080,\quad
  \pi_5 = T_{\rm ep}\sqrt{\frac{a_{\max}}{D_g}} = 4.70.
\]

\paragraph{Meaningful recombinations.}
\[
  T_{\rm tr} = 2\sqrt{D_g/a_{\max}} = 2.68\,\text{s},\qquad
  \tau = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi_5 = \frac{T_{\rm ep}}{T_{\rm tr}} = 2.35,
\]
\[
  N_s = \frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_3}}
      = \frac{T_{\rm tr}}{T_s} = 1.60\;\text{(slosh cycles/transit)},\qquad
  \zeta = \frac{\pi_4}{2\sqrt{\pi_1/\pi_3}} = \frac{c}{2\omega_n} = 0.008,
\]
\[
  \theta_{\rm ss} = \frac{\pi_2\,\pi_3}{\pi_1}
    = \frac{\eta\,a_{\max}\,L}{g} = 0.071\;\text{rad}.
\]

$\theta_{\rm ss}$ is the equilibrium slosh angle under sustained full thrust.
No tolerance parameter appears in its formula, yet $\theta_{\rm ss} \approx
\epsilon_\theta = 0.07$\,rad --- a collision between the dynamics and the
success condition that falls directly out of the $\pi$ groups. It means
any final approach at full commanded acceleration violates the slosh
constraint; near-goal corrections must be gentle.

Sweep of blind nominal PD vs.\ oracle (PD gains divided by $\kappa$) at

\begin{center}\small

\midrule
0.65 & 0\%   & 100\% \\
0.70 & 0\%   & 100\% \\
0.75 & 25.5\% & 100\% \\

The gap is not a transit-timeout effect (condition \eqref{eq:gain_cond}
requires $\tau < 1.24$, but $\tau=2.35$). It arises through settling: at
$\kappa<1$ the actuator delivers $\kappa$-attenuated corrections in the
near-goal linear regime, slowing convergence in position, velocity, and
slosh simultaneously. The oracle compensates by scaling its command by
$1/\kappa$, restoring the intended correction magnitude.

This gap satisfies the minimum requirement: blind vs.\ oracle separation
exceeds 20\% across $\kappa \in \{0.65, 0.80\}$, with oracle at 100\% for
all $\kappa \geq 0.65$.

\subsection*{Open questions / next steps}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Confirm gap at $\tau \approx 1.4$.} Implement the recommended
    \texttt{max\_steps} and unknown ranges; run blind, oracle, and
    probe-then-adapt baselines on 200 seeds.
  \item \textbf{Design probe-then-adapt oracle.} The hand-written oracle
    currently adjusts gains only; it does not plan trajectories. For action

  \item \textbf{First GRPO run.} Once the gap is confirmed: use same config

    to write a probe in turn 1 and adapt in turn 2.
  \item \textbf{Does the model learn to probe at all?} The key empirical
    question: does the trained model's turn-1 function look like the
    canonical diagnostic pulse, or does it collapse to a fixed controller?
    The turn-1 reward curve (mean reward of turn-1 draft against training step)
    is the primary diagnostic.
\end{enumerate}
